### COLD OPEN — THE BEFORE (0:00–1:15)

Early in my career, as a developer, personal data was just a field on a form. A name. An email address. A "client" — whatever that meant that week. I wrote validation rules, I made sure the database didn't break, and I moved on to the next ticket. It never once occurred to me to ask what happened to that data five years later, or whether anyone could still explain why we had it.

I wasn't careless. I was doing the job as I understood it: make the system work. Nobody had taught me that a name in a field was a person with a right to ask, one day, "why do you still have this?" — and that if nobody in the organisation could answer, that wasn't a technical gap. It was a liability with a real person's name attached to it.

If you'd asked me back then what my job was protecting, I'd have said "the data." I didn't yet understand that the data wasn't the thing at risk. The person it described was.

### THE TURNING POINT (1:15–4:00)

That changed the moment the stakes stopped being abstract.

I moved into managing clinical and health data across research sites in West Africa — over 285,000 people, vaccination records, patient histories. And almost immediately I ran into the same kind of sloppy field I'd built as a developer, except this time it wasn't a "client" — it was a child's vaccination date, recorded three different ways across three sites, because nobody had ever agreed what the date was actually supposed to mean.

Same mistake. Completely different weight. Get a client field wrong and you get an awkward invoice. Get a vaccination date wrong and a region gets marked as under-vaccinated when it isn't — or the other way round, and a health programme thinks it's done more than it has.

I remember sitting with that realisation for longer than felt comfortable. Not because the fix was hard — the fix was, eventually, a data dictionary and a training session. What sat uncomfortably was the recognition that I'd made this exact category of mistake before, in a context where the worst outcome was an awkward email, and never once stopped to ask what the same habit would cost in a context where the worst outcome was a child left off a vaccination programme.

That was the moment the abstraction broke for me. I started asking a question I'd never asked as a developer: not just "does this field work," but "can I explain, right now, to the person this data belongs to, why we have it and what we're doing with it?" If the answer was no, that wasn't a technical problem to fix later. It was the actual problem.

We fixed it the unglamorous way — a data dictionary, written definitions, training every site to capture the same thing the same way, and a very uncomfortable meeting where three sites who all believed they were doing it correctly had to agree on one shared answer. Nobody thanked me for that meeting. It wasn't the kind of work that photographs well. But it was the first time I understood that "define your terms clearly" wasn't a nice-to-have. For data about real people, it was the whole job.

### THE AFTER (4:00–7:30)

I carried that question straight into the job I'm in now. Not long after I started in UK higher education, I noticed my own team fielding requests for "all of it" — full extracts of student and staff data, no scope, no stated purpose, handed over because saying yes was quicker than asking why.

The old me — the developer who just made the system work — would have processed that request without blinking. It's a technically simple task. Run the export, send the file, close the ticket.

Instead, I stopped it. We introduced a data access request policy: state what you need, state why, we run a needs assessment against that, and you get exactly that. It's a small policy. It sits behind the scenes; nobody outside the data team ever sees it get enforced. But it exists because I'm no longer the person who thinks "the system works" is the same thing as "we can justify holding this" — and that shift, from one to the other, is the entire difference between an organisation that manages personal data and one that's quietly collecting a fine it doesn't know about yet.

### WHAT IT MEANS FOR YOU (7:30–10:30)

If you're where I was — treating personal data as just a field, a technical object, something the system handles so you don't have to think about it — here's the honest version of what's ahead. That gap doesn't announce itself. Nothing breaks. No error appears on screen. It just sits there, quietly, until someone with the authority to ask "why do you have this" finally does — a regulator, an auditor, or a person exercising their own legal right to ask what you're holding on them.

The organisations that get fined millions under GDPR aren't usually the reckless ones. They're the ones that never had anyone stop and ask the question early, the way nobody made me ask it as a developer. The fix isn't a bigger legal team. It's one habit: for every piece of personal data you touch, know what it's for, and know when it stops being yours to hold.

You don't need my job title to start doing that. You need the willingness to ask the uncomfortable question in a meeting, the way I finally did — the one that stops a request for "everything" in its tracks, even when it would be so much easier to just say yes. That's genuinely the whole shift. Not a certification. Not a bigger budget. Just refusing to let "the system can technically do this" stand in for "we've actually thought about whether we should."

If there's a version of you still writing the client field, still closing the ticket without asking what happens to the data in five years — I'm not telling you that to make you feel behind. I'm telling you because I was exactly that person, for longer than I'd like to admit, and the only thing that changed it was the stakes finally being high enough that I couldn't look away from the question anymore. You don't have to wait for your own version of that moment. You can just start asking now.
