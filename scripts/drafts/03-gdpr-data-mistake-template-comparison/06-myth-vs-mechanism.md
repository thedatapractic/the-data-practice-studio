### THE HOOK — THE TURNING POINT (0:00–0:45)

On the 25th of May 2018, a new law took effect across Europe that most organisations, seven-plus years later, still fundamentally misunderstand.

GDPR didn't arrive quietly. There were campaigns, countdown emails, entire law firms built overnight around it. Every company with a European customer spent that spring rewriting its privacy policy and hoping that was enough. And in the years since, regulators across Europe have handed out some of the largest data protection fines in corporate history — numbers with enough zeros that they made the news on their own, sitting alongside the details of exactly what the company got wrong.

Here's what almost nobody takes away from those headlines correctly.

### THE REVEAL (0:45–1:30)

Everyone assumes those massive fines happen because a company got hacked — a breach, stolen records, a villain breaking in from outside.

I don't believe that's the real story. Not because breaches don't happen — they do, and they get punished. But the pattern underneath most of the largest fines isn't a hacker at all. It's an organisation that simply couldn't answer a basic question about its own data: what do we hold, why do we hold it, and when were we supposed to stop? That's not a security failure. It's a governance failure, and it was sitting there quietly, years before any regulator came knocking.

Security failures announce themselves. Something breaks, an alarm fires, someone gets a call at midnight. Governance failures don't. They just sit there, invisible, doing nothing dramatic at all — right up until the day someone with real authority finally asks the one question the organisation was never prepared to answer.

### THE PRECEDENT (1:30–5:30)

I've watched this pattern up close, more than once.

Not long after I started in UK higher education, I noticed my own team fielding requests for "all of it" — full extracts of student and staff data, no scope, no stated purpose, handed over because saying yes was quicker than asking why. Nobody involved was reckless. Nobody was trying to break a law. It was simply the default, because no one had ever been assigned the job of asking the question out loud.

So I stopped it. We introduced a data access request policy: state what you need, state why, we run a needs assessment against that, and you get exactly that — not the whole database, because asking for everything used to be easier than asking properly. One of the very first requests to hit that new policy was for a full extract of former students' contact details, "just in case it's useful." Under the old habit, that goes out the same afternoon. Under the new one, it doesn't go out at all, because nobody in the room could give a reason for it beyond "it might be useful someday" — which is precisely the sentence that turns into a fine, years later, once enough of them pile up unnoticed.

That single habit — extending trust by default instead of by justification — is precisely the mechanism regulators are built to find. Years earlier, in a completely different context, I managed clinical data across research sites in West Africa, where vaccination dates were recorded three different ways because nobody had agreed what the date actually meant. Different domain, wildly different stakes, same root cause underneath: nobody had been made to answer, clearly and continuously, what a piece of personal data was for. We only caught it because the consequences were immediate and visible — a region's vaccine coverage numbers didn't add up. Most organisations don't get that early warning. Their version of the same mistake just sits quietly in a spreadsheet, unnoticed, for years.

Neither of those moments involved a hacker. Both of them were exactly the kind of gap that, left alone in an organisation with millions of customer or citizen records instead of a few thousand, is what regulators are actually punishing when the fine finally lands.

### THE MECHANISM, COMPRESSED (5:30–7:00)

Here's the whole thing in one sentence you could repeat to a colleague: if the same person's data exists in five systems and nobody's sure which one's correct, that's not five records. That's one person you can no longer honestly answer "what do we hold on you" about.

Every fine, underneath the legal language, comes back to that same broken sentence, repeated thousands of times across an organisation's systems. Purpose nobody re-checks. Retention nobody enforces. Access nobody scopes. None of it looks dramatic from the inside. It looks like Tuesday. That's exactly why it survives so long before anyone notices — there's no alarm, no error message, just a slow accumulation of unanswered questions until the day someone with real authority finally asks one.

You can test this on your own organisation in under a minute, and you don't need to be a data manager to do it. Ask anyone — genuinely anyone — "if I asked you what personal data of mine you're holding right now, and why, could you actually tell me?" Watch the pause before the answer. That pause is the mechanism. It's the same pause, at a much bigger scale, that shows up in a regulator's investigation report.

### LANDING (7:00–9:00)

This isn't going away, and it isn't going to get softer. Regulators have spent the years since 2018 getting better at exactly this kind of investigation — not looking for a break-in, looking for the paper trail of decisions an organisation never actually made. The organisations that get caught out next won't be the reckless ones. They'll be the ordinary ones who assumed "we haven't been hacked" meant "we're fine," right up until someone asked them a question their own systems couldn't answer.

I'll say the unfashionable part plainly: this has very little to do with the law, and everything to do with a discipline most organisations never built in the first place. GDPR didn't invent the obligation to know what you're holding and why. It just put a fine on the organisations that never bothered to find out.

Here's what to do with that. This week, pick one piece of personal data your own team holds, and answer three questions about it, honestly: what is it, why do we have it, and when does it stop being ours to hold? If any one of those makes you pause, you've just found the exact gap regulators are trained to look for — and you found it before they did.

Do that with genuine honesty, not the version of honesty that protects your own team's reputation, and you'll very likely find at least one answer you don't like. That's not a failure on your part. It's the first accurate map anyone's drawn of that particular gap — and an accurate map, however uncomfortable, is worth more than the comfortable assumption it replaces.
