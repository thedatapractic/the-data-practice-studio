### THE HOOK (0:00–0:20)

Here's the direct answer: most companies don't get fined millions under GDPR because of hackers. They get fined because nobody in the organisation could say what personal data they held, why they were holding it, or when it was supposed to be deleted. That's it. That's the mistake, and it's rarely dramatic — it just quietly accumulates until someone with real authority finally asks the question. I'm going to show you exactly what it looks like, why it's so easy to fall into without meaning to, and how to check whether your own organisation is already sitting on it.

### CONTEXT (0:20–1:00)

This matters because most people, understandably, picture the wrong villain. They picture a breach — someone breaking in, records leaked, a headline. That does happen. But a huge share of the largest data protection penalties involve no hacker at all. They involve an organisation that simply lost track of its own data, which is a much quieter, much more common problem, and one that's sitting inside plenty of organisations that have never had anything "hacked" in their lives. It's also a problem that never shows up on a dashboard, never triggers an alert, and can sit there for years before anyone with the authority to ask about it actually does.

I'm Aji Saine — I manage statutory data reporting for a UK university, where this exact risk is part of my actual job, not a hypothetical. I've also built the policies that catch it before it becomes one.

### THE BREAKDOWN

**Purpose (1:00–3:00).** GDPR requires that personal data is only collected for a specific, stated reason. In practice, that reason gets written down once, at collection, and then quietly forgotten. A job application form, a customer enquiry, an event sign-up — the purpose is clear on day one. Nobody revisits it. Two years later the data's still sitting there, and nobody can say what it's currently for, only what it was originally for. Picture a competition sign-up: the purpose was "notify the winner," fulfilled in a week — but the list was too useful to delete, so it quietly became part of the general mailing database instead, without anyone re-asking whether that was ever agreed to.

**Retention (3:00–5:00).** This is the twin of purpose, and the one almost nobody enforces: you shouldn't keep personal data longer than that purpose requires. Deleting things feels risky, so the default in most organisations is to keep everything, indefinitely, "just in case." I've seen this directly — early on at the university, my own team was fielding requests for full data extracts with no stated purpose and no expiry date in mind, purely because asking for everything was quicker than asking properly. That's the default almost everywhere, until someone decides otherwise, and most retention policies exist only as a document — not as anything a system actually enforces.

**Ownership (5:00–6:45).** Here's the actual root cause underneath both of those. Ask yourself: in your organisation, who decides how long a customer's data is kept after the relationship ends? Who decides which systems it's allowed to live in? Most of the time, honestly, nobody. It's whatever the system defaulted to years ago. And when a regulator investigates, they don't usually find one dramatic villain — they find dozens of small, unowned decisions that quietly added up to personal data sitting somewhere it should never have stayed. One of the clearest signs of this gap: ask an organisation to produce everything it holds on one named individual — a request anyone can legally make — and see how many different systems someone has to check before they can even attempt an honest answer.

### PRACTICAL APPLICATION (6:15–8:00)

So what do you actually do with this tomorrow? Three things, in order.

Find out what personal data your team or system holds — genuinely holds, not what the policy document claims. That usually means someone actually opening the systems and looking, not trusting a diagram drawn three years ago.

Attach a specific, current reason to each category of it. Not the reason it was collected originally, if that's expired — the reason you're still holding it today. If you can't write that reason down in a sentence, that's your answer.

And set an actual deletion point, one that's enforced by a person or a process, not just written down in a policy and forgotten. That's the entire discipline a regulator is checking for, stripped of the legal language, and it doesn't need new software to start. It needs one person spending an afternoon actually looking, and the authority to act on what they find.

### RECAP + CTA (8:00–9:30)

So: the mistake that gets companies fined millions isn't usually a hacker. It's treating personal data as something to collect and keep by default, instead of something you have to justify holding at every point in its life. Purpose, retention, ownership — three plain words, and almost every fine you've ever read a headline about traces back to one of them being nobody's job.

Try this today. Pick one system you can actually see into, find one field of personal data, and ask: what's it for, and when does it get deleted? If you can't answer both in under a minute, you've found exactly the kind of gap that gets organisations fined — and you found it before a regulator, or an auditor, or the person the data belongs to, did.
