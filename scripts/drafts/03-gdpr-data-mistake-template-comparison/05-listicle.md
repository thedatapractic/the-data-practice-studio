### THE HOOK (0:00–0:20)

Five signs your organisation is one audit away from a GDPR fine. Number four is the one I've seen most often — and it's rarely the one people expect. The order isn't random.

### QUICK SETUP (0:20–0:45)

I'm Aji Saine, I manage statutory data reporting for a UK university, and none of these five things are hacker-related. Every one of them is a quiet governance habit, sitting in plenty of organisations that have never had a breach in their lives, never set off a single security alert, and still end up explaining themselves to a regulator. To qualify for this list, a habit had to be one I've genuinely seen more than once, in more than one organisation — nothing here is hypothetical. Let's go.

### THE ITEMS

**One — nobody can say why you're holding it (0:45–2:15).**
Pick any piece of personal data in your systems and ask: what's it currently for? Not what it was for when it was collected two years ago — what it's for right now. If the honest answer is "I'm not sure, but it might be useful," that's sign one. It's the most common gap I've seen, and it's almost never malicious. It's just a decision nobody ever made. Picture a competition sign-up list from three years back — the prize was handed out, the purpose was done, and the list just quietly became part of the newsletter database because deleting it felt like a waste.

**Two — nothing has an actual expiry date (2:15–3:45).**
Retention policies exist on paper in most organisations. What's rarer is a system that actually enforces one. "Temporary" exports that have quietly lived on a shared drive for four years. Old customer records nobody's deleted because deleting feels riskier than keeping. If your policy says twelve months and your systems have no idea what that means, the policy isn't protecting you — it's just a document that makes everyone feel better until someone actually checks.

**Three — anyone can ask for "everything" and get it (3:45–5:15).**
This one used to happen on my own team before I stopped it. A request comes in for a full extract, no scope, no stated purpose — and because saying yes is faster than asking why, it gets fulfilled. Every one of those unscoped exports is a new place personal data now lives, that nobody's tracking, that nobody scoped, that nobody remembers exists in eighteen months. Multiply that by every well-meaning "can you just send me everything" request over a few years, and you get an organisation that genuinely cannot list all the places one person's data lives.

**Four is the one I see in almost every organisation I've ever worked in or advised, regardless of size or sector.**

**Four — the same person's data lives in five systems, and nobody's sure which one's correct (5:15–7:15).**
A customer updates their address once. It updates in one system. The other four still have the old one. Now you've got five versions of one person's data, no clear "source of truth," and if that person asks what you hold on them, you can't actually answer accurately — you'd have to check five places and hope they agree. That's not a technical inconvenience. That's the exact question a regulator asks, and most organisations fail it quietly, every single day, without ever finding out. It usually starts innocently: a new system gets bought, someone exports a copy of customer data to "seed" it, and nobody ever draws the line connecting the copy back to the original.

**Five — no single person's job is to say yes or no to any of this (7:15–8:45).**
This is the one underneath all the others. Ask yourself: who in your organisation actually has the authority, and the responsibility, to say "no, we're not keeping that" or "no, you can't have the full export"? If the honest answer is nobody, then none of the first four signs are surprising — they're just what happens by default when no one owns the decision. Every organisation I've worked in that fixed this started the same way: one named person, with real authority, whose job was specifically to keep asking the question everyone else was too busy to ask.

### RECAP + CTA (7:45–9:30)

So: can't explain why you're holding it, nothing expires, anyone can get everything, the same person exists five times over, and nobody's actually accountable for any of it. Any one of those on its own is a bad habit. All five together is exactly the pattern regulators find when they hand out the fines that make headlines — and notice, not one of the five involved a hacker.

Go check your own team against this list — honestly, not defensively. Nobody who built these habits was trying to break the law; they were just doing what was easiest at the time, the way most habits get built. If you found yourself nodding at more than two, that's not a reason to panic. It's a reason to start with the one you can actually fix this week, because every single one of these five is fixable without buying new software — they're all decisions somebody just needs to actually make, and then keep making.
